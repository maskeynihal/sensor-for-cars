HJ60 coolant temperature gauge: how the circuit works

Based on the Toyota FSM pages 13-73 / 13-74 (BJ, HJ series) and your measurements. 12 V truck, single battery. Click any part of the drawing, or step through the tour.

1. Two parts, in plain words

The sender (in the engine)

A brass plug screwed into the cylinder head, with its tip sitting in the coolant. Inside is a thermistor: a resistor whose value changes with temperature.

Hotter coolant → lower resistance. FSM: 226 Ω at 50 °C, 26 Ω at 115 °C.

It has one wire (yellow-green, "YG"). The other side of the circuit is the engine block itself (ground). It doesn't produce a voltage. It just changes how easily current flows.

"Sender" because it sends the temperature to the dash, as a resistance.

The gauge (on the dash)

The needle you see. Behind it is a bimetal strip (two metals bonded together that bend when warmed) wrapped in a tiny heater wire (25 Ω, terminals E and F).

More current through the heater → strip warms → bends → needle moves toward H.

It's slow on purpose: it takes seconds to warm up or cool down, so it averages the current.

The voltage regulator (hidden inside the fuel gauge)

A tiny bimetal switch that clicks on and off by itself, a little like a car's indicator flasher. Battery voltage goes from 12.9 V (engine off) to 14 V (running). Without the regulator the needle would rise every time you started the engine.

The regulator switches on and off so that the average is about 7 V, whatever the battery is doing. The gauge averages the pulses, so the needle is steady.

This is why your multimeter jumped around: it was reading a voltage that switches on and off.

5. How the ESP32 reads it

Why two wires, not one

The gauge heater (25 Ω) and the sender sit in series. The feed voltage splits between them like water through two pipes. The share that lands across the sender depends only on the two resistances:

k = VF ÷ VE = Rs ÷ (Rs + 25)

So the ESP32 reads both E and F at the same moment, throws away the moments when the regulator is off, and computes Rs = 25 × k ÷ (1 − k), then °C from the sender curve. The battery voltage and the pulsing cancel out.

Why it doesn't upset the dash gauge

Each tap goes through a 68 kΩ + 10 kΩ divider: thousands of times more resistance than the gauge circuit. It draws well under 1% of the current, so the needle doesn't move.

The divider also shrinks the voltage by 7.8×, so 14 V becomes 1.8 V, safe for the ESP32 (max 3.3 V).

Pin A is wrong: it's before the regulator, so it stays on while the sender wire drops to 0 V. Try "ESP32 tapped at pin A" above.

6. Words used

WordMeaning here
SenderThe temperature sensor in the engine. A resistor that changes with heat.
Gauge / receiverThe needle on the dash. The FSM calls it the "receiver gauge".
BimetalTwo metals stuck together. Heat makes one grow more, so the strip bends.
NTC thermistorNegative temperature coefficient: resistance goes down as temperature goes up.
E, FThe two terminals of the temperature gauge's heater. E = feed side, F = sender side.
Pin AThe cluster connector pin that feeds the regulator. Before the regulator, so not the one to tap.
GroundThe metal of the truck (body, engine block), which acts as the return wire back to the battery.
DividerTwo resistors that scale a voltage down by a fixed ratio.
Ratio kSender voltage ÷ feed voltage. Depends only on temperature, not on battery or pulsing.
